My research asks how political competition shapes democratic representation. I am particularly interested in the relationship between citizens and political parties, the structure of party systems, and what happens when political choices fail to reflect the preferences of voters.

These substantive questions are closely connected to my work in political methodology. Much of my research therefore moves back and forth between questions about democracy and representation and questions about how we measure political preferences, ideology, and party competition across countries.

## Democratic Representation and Resilience

A central theme of my research is the quality of political representation. How well do political parties represent the preferences of citizens? What happens when voters cannot find a party that reflects their views? And how do citizens respond when political representation becomes weak or distorted?

My work with Seth Jolly and Jonathan Polk has examined multidimensional voter-party incongruence, political dissatisfaction, support for anti-establishment parties, and vote switching. Our current work follows voters who abandon their previous party and asks whether their new choice produces a better ideological match or instead reflects broader dissatisfaction with the political alternatives available to them.

More broadly, I am interested in the conditions under which democratic political systems remain responsive and resilient when representation becomes strained.

## Parties, Polarization, and Political Competition

A second strand of my research examines how political conflict becomes organized into party systems.

This work asks whether political competition is structured around coherent ideological dimensions, how those dimensions differ across countries and regions, and how party systems change as new political conflicts emerge. My research has examined economic and cultural competition, European integration, immigration, urban-rural divisions, issue salience, and the development of new political cleavages.

Recent projects extend these questions beyond Western Europe, including research on party-system structuration in the Global South and the development of multidimensional political competition across very different democratic contexts.

Much of this research draws on data from the **[Chapel Hill Expert Survey](/ches/)**, for which I have served as Principal Investigator since 2008.

## International Relations, Conflict, and Human Rights

A longstanding strand of my research examines political violence, human rights, and the interaction between domestic and international politics.

With Daniel Hill, Ryan Welch, and the late Will Moore, I am completing a book manuscript, **Dissidents and Deadly Decisions: A Contentious Politics Account of Domestic Terror**. The project develops a "web of contention" approach to political violence, treating terror as one tactical choice available to dissident groups alongside nonviolent protest and other forms of violence.

Rather than explaining terror primarily through broad structural conditions, we focus on strategic interaction: how dissident groups respond to the behavior of governments and other challengers, and how political institutions and international conflict shape those choices. The project combines global cross-national analysis with group-level analysis of political violence in India.

I have also worked on the measurement of human rights, as a founding member of the Human Rights Measurement Initiative (HRMI), including research on practitioner-based measures of civil and political rights. Other work examines social unrest, international threats, and the ways in which external conflict reshapes domestic political competition.

## Urban Democracy in Africa

An emerging part of my research examines democratic resilience from the perspective of everyday urban life.

The **Urban Democracy in Africa** project studies how democratic practices are sustained in cities facing economic inequality, institutional strain, and overlapping social and political crises. The project focuses on cities in Botswana, Zambia, Malawi, and Ghana and asks how social relationships, local institutions, neighborhood organization, and everyday forms of cooperation contribute to democratic resilience.

Rather than treating democracy only as a set of national institutions, this work examines how democratic practices are produced and maintained through local social networks, civic interaction, and the shared use of urban space.

This project broadens my research on representation by connecting party politics and political institutions to the everyday social foundations of democratic life.

## Political Measurement and Expert Surveys

My methodological research focuses on the measurement problems that arise when scholars attempt to compare political preferences, ideological positions, and party competition across individuals and political contexts.

A substantial part of this work has involved developing and applying methods for placing parties and voters in common ideological spaces. My research has used Bayesian scaling, multidimensional scaling, anchoring vignettes, latent-variable models, and other approaches to political measurement.

My current work also turns the measurement problem back onto expert surveys themselves. How many experts are needed to produce reliable estimates of party positions? Which survey questions provide genuinely distinct information? Can expert surveys be made substantially shorter without sacrificing measurement quality?

These projects use the accumulated experience and data of CHES to study expert surveys not simply as sources of political data, but as measurement instruments whose properties can themselves be analyzed and improved.

## Bayesian and Quantitative Methods

I have a longstanding interest in Bayesian statistics, quantitative research design, and latent-variable modeling.

I have taught Bayesian methods at the ICPSR Summer Program, the Essex Summer School in Social Science Data Analysis, the Global School in Empirical Research Methods at the University of St. Gallen, and in workshops and courses at universities in Europe and the United States.

My methodological work includes Bayesian approaches to scaling and latent-variable models as well as broader questions of measurement, cross-national comparability, and research design.

## Current Projects

My current collaborative projects include:

- **Voter-party congruence and electoral switching**: examining where disaffected voters go when they leave their previous party and whether switching improves political representation.
- **Party-system structuration in the Global South**: studying whether political competition is organized into coherent ideological dimensions outside established Western democracies.
- **Dissidents and Deadly Decisions: A Contentious Politics Account of Domestic Terror**. Book manuscript in progress.
- **Urban democracy and democratic resilience in Africa**: examining how local institutions, social relationships, and urban practices sustain democratic life under conditions of inequality and political strain.
- **Multidimensional polarization**: studying how polarization develops across multiple ideological dimensions and how those dimensions interact within party systems.
- **The salience of issue salience**: comparing alternative approaches to measuring which political issues parties emphasize.
- **How many experts are enough?**: examining the relationship between the number of expert respondents and the reliability and precision of expert-survey estimates.
- **Simplifying expert surveys**: identifying redundant survey items and determining whether expert surveys can be shortened without meaningful information loss.
- **National and local representation in the United States**: studying how the relationship between citizens and political representatives varies across levels of government.

For published work related to these projects, see my **[Publications](/publications/)**.
